The problem modelling solves

A company has 400 tables copied from six systems. An analyst wants monthly revenue by customer segment. To get it they must know which of the three orders tables is authoritative, that cancelled orders have status = 9, that the EU subsidiary reports in euros, and that test accounts must be excluded using a rule someone explained in Slack in 2024.

They work it out and build a dashboard. Someone else does the same thing next month and gets a different number, because they excluded test accounts differently.

Modelling is the discipline of doing that work once, in one place, so the answer is consistent and the knowledge is in code rather than in people's heads.

Layers

Every mature platform organises tables into layers. The names differ — bronze/silver/gold, raw/staging/marts, landing/base/reporting — and the idea is identical.

Three rules make this work:

  • Raw is sacred. Never transform it in place. It is your ability to rebuild everything, and your evidence of what the source actually said.
  • Each layer reads only from the layer above. No mart querying raw directly — that is how logic gets duplicated and then diverges.
  • Business logic lives in one layer. If "active customer" is defined in four marts, you will get four numbers in four dashboards, and a meeting about which one is right that produces no useful outcome.

Dimensional modelling

The dominant way to model analytics data, from Ralph Kimball. Two kinds of table:

  • Facts — things that happened. Long and narrow: mostly numbers and foreign keys. fct_orders, fct_page_views.
  • Dimensions — the context around those things. Short and wide: mostly descriptive text. dim_customer, dim_product, dim_date.

It has survived thirty years of fashion for two reasons. It is fast, because the join graph is one hop deep. And non-engineers can understand it — an analyst can look at a star schema and work out how to answer their question without asking you, which is worth more than any performance benefit.

Grain is the concept to get right

The grain is what exactly one row of a fact table represents. Not "orders" — "one row per order line item". Declare it explicitly, in writing, before you write any SQL.

Almost every dimensional modelling disaster traces back to a fact table with an ambiguous or mixed grain. If some rows are orders and some are line items, then summing revenue double-counts, and the error is invisible until someone reconciles against finance.

The test: can you state the grain in one sentence beginning "one row per…"? If not, the table is not ready to be built.

A note on normalisation

OLTP databases normalise — third normal form, no duplicated values — to avoid update anomalies, because they are written to constantly. Analytics tables are written once and read thousands of times, so the trade-off inverts: analytics deliberately denormalises. Storage is cheap, joins are expensive, and an analyst reading country_name directly rather than joining three tables to find it is a genuine productivity gain.

If you come from application development, this will feel wrong for about a month.

Slowly changing dimensions

A customer moves from Berlin to Munich. Last year's orders — were they placed by a Berlin customer or a Munich one?

Both answers are defensible, and you must choose deliberately.

TypeWhat it doesResultUse for
Type 1Overwrite the old valueOnly the current truth exists; history is rewrittenCorrecting typos, fields where history is meaningless
Type 2Add a new row with validity datesBoth versions exist; facts join to the version that was current at the timeAnything where historical reporting must reflect the world as it was

A Type 2 dimension looks like this:

Note the two different keys. customer_id is the business key — the real customer. customer_key is a surrogate key identifying this version of them. Facts join on the surrogate key, which is what makes a 2024 order correctly attribute to Berlin. This is what people mean by point-in-time correct, and it matters enormously for both historical reporting and machine learning training data.

Type 2 costs complexity, so apply it only where history genuinely matters. Ask the business rather than guessing — and be aware that "do you need to see what this looked like last year?" gets a more accurate answer than "do you want Type 2?".

Incremental patterns

How you update a table when new data arrives. The choice interacts directly with idempotency from Reliability.

PatternHowUse whenIdempotent?
Full refreshRebuild the whole tableSmall tables, complex logic, correctness over costYes, inherently
AppendInsert new rows onlyImmutable events that never changeNo — needs a dedup key
Insert-overwriteReplace whole partitionsRecords within a period can changeYes
Merge / upsertMatch on key, update or insertMutable records with a reliable key (CDC output)Yes
Keep the ability to rebuild

Whatever incremental pattern you adopt, make sure a full rebuild is still possible and occasionally tested. Incremental logic drifts — a bug shipped three months ago leaves a permanent dent in history that only a rebuild will remove.

Some teams schedule a full rebuild monthly for exactly this reason. It costs one expensive night and buys certainty that incremental output still matches what the logic says it should be.

Lambda and Kappa, briefly

Two named architectures you will hear about, worth recognising:

  • Lambda — run a batch layer (accurate, slow) and a speed layer (fast, approximate) in parallel and merge them at query time. Its flaw is famous: two implementations of the same business logic, which inevitably drift apart.
  • Kappa — one streaming pipeline handles everything; reprocessing means replaying the log from the start. One codebase, but it demands long log retention and real streaming maturity.

Most teams land somewhere pragmatic: batch for the bulk of analytics, one or two streaming paths where the business case is clear. Knowing the names and Lambda's dual-implementation flaw is enough — and note the nuance in Worked examples, where a Lambda-shaped design is acceptable precisely because the two paths compute different things and so cannot drift.

Checklist

  • Explain what each layer does and why marts must not read from raw
  • Sort a list of tables into facts and dimensions, and justify each
  • State the grain of a fact table in one sentence, and explain what goes wrong when it is mixed
  • Explain why analytics denormalises when application databases normalise
  • Choose between SCD Type 1 and Type 2 for a given field, based on a question you would ask the business
  • Explain what a surrogate key does in a Type 2 dimension that a business key cannot
  • Pick an incremental pattern for a table and say whether it is idempotent as written
